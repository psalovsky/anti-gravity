# EM-TIME-001 — Emergent time and the frozen-ice challenge

**Recorded:** 2026-11-02  
**Status:** open conceptual research problem; no new experiment claimed  
**Area:** foundations of quantum mechanics, thermodynamics, and quantum gravity  
**Origin:** discussion of an October 2026 cold-atom experiment and the objection that frozen ice appears not to change.

## 1. The problem in ordinary language

The research question arose from the claim that time can *emerge* from internal changes in a physical system, rather than being supplied by an independent external clock.

**The challenge posed in our discussion:** A block of frozen ice looks static. It does not melt or visibly reorganize and may remain in thermal equilibrium for a long period. If change or increasing entropy creates time, should time stop for the ice? Yet we can leave the ice untouched for an hour, return, and say that an hour has passed. What, precisely, was supposed to have stopped?

This is a useful counterexample to an overly broad statement such as "no visible change = no time." It motivates a more exact distinction between the existence of time, the availability of an internal clock, and the thermodynamic arrow of time.

**Core research problem:** When an internal entropy-based clock ceases to advance, has physical time ceased, or has only a particular observable lost its ability to parameterize the system's evolution?

## 2. What motivated the discussion

A 2026 study by Giovanni Barontini, *Testing the problem of time with cold atoms*, used approximately 24,000 ultracold atoms in a controlled, largely isolated two-region system. The distribution of atoms in an observed region could be used to define a form of *entropic time*; the system underwent cycles analogous to expansion and contraction. This is an operational construction of an internal clock: changes within the system can replace external laboratory time for a useful description of some dynamics.

**What was not demonstrated:** literal creation of time, stoppage of laboratory time, proof that the universe has no fundamental time, or a connection to gravity shielding. A particular internal time variable can stop advancing when the observable used to define it ceases to change.

Source: [University of Birmingham / ScienceDaily, October 30, 2026](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/10/261028234703.htm).  
Paper: [G. Barontini, *Testing the problem of time with cold atoms*, Physical Review Research (2026), DOI 10.1103/1h9j-df4k](https://doi.org/10.1103/1h9j-df4k).

## 3. Why frozen ice is not a timeless system

| Concept | Frozen ice | Meaning for the time question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Macroscopic appearance | A crystal retains approximately the same shape and phase. | No visible change does not imply absence of microscopic dynamics. |
| Microscopic dynamics | At nonzero temperature, atoms and molecules vibrate in the lattice and exchange energy. | There can be evolution and correlations even when the bulk looks unchanged. |
| Thermodynamic equilibrium | Macroscopic averages may be stationary; net entropy production may be approximately zero. | *Zero entropy production* is not *zero entropy*, and neither automatically means "no time." |
| Quantum stationary state | Some isolated states have time-independent probabilities for stationary observables. | An external time parameter may still exist in the mathematical description; that state may simply provide no usable internal clock. |
| Absolute-zero idealization | Cooling reduces thermal motion, but quantum zero-point fluctuations can remain. | Zero-point fluctuations are **not automatically a ticking clock**. One must specify an observable that actually changes. |

An important additional distinction: the fine-grained von Neumann entropy of an isolated quantum system remains constant under unitary evolution, even though its quantum state and measurable correlations can change. Therefore, an entropy-based time must specify **which entropy**, what coarse-graining, which subsystem, and why the chosen observable orders events.

## 4. Four different claims that must not be confused

1. **Operational time:** A subsystem can serve as a clock if an observable changes in a reliably ordered way.
2. **Thermodynamic arrow:** Entropy production and coarse-grained irreversibility can distinguish past from future under appropriate conditions.
3. **Fundamental time:** A theoretical model may or may not contain time as a primitive variable.
4. **Observability of change:** If absolutely nothing internal changes, observers inside that system have no internal experiment with which to distinguish durations.

The fourth statement does not prove the third. "We cannot measure elapsed time from inside" and "elapsed time does not exist" are different propositions.

### Frozen-ice thought experiment

Imagine three systems:
- **A — Ice:** a crystal in equilibrium, with stable macroscopic appearance but microscopic fluctuations and correlations.
- **B — Quantum clock:** a controlled cold-atom subsystem whose particle distribution defines an entropic clock.
- **C — External reference:** independent laboratory timing available to an experimental observer.

If the chosen observable in B becomes constant while C continues, an investigator should test whether *other* internal observables or two-time correlation functions still evolve. Similarly, A can appear unchanged while its microscopic dynamics remain measurable. A frozen crystal is a challenge to a simplistic definition of emergent time, not a demonstration that the approach fails.

If **all** possible internal observables and correlations were exactly unchanged, the issue becomes deeper: there may be no internal operational way to assign a duration, while competing theories disagree about whether a fundamental time parameter is still meaningful.

## 5. Specific open questions

**Q1 — Clock dependence.** Can two internal clock variables disagree, for example when the entropy-based variable is flat but a phase or correlation-based variable changes? What makes one clock physically privileged?

**Q2 — Entropy versus dynamics.** Can a system have approximately zero net entropy production and still undergo observable internal dynamics? (Standard reversible or equilibrium examples suggest yes.) How is this reflected in the cold-atom construction?

**Q3 — Stationarity versus time.** In a true stationary quantum state, which relational observables could serve as a clock, if any? Is a global phase physically observable?

**Q4 — Recurrences and reversals.** If an observable reverses direction or repeats, how can an internal time variable remain unambiguous and monotonic across the complete cycle?

**Q5 — Universe versus subsystem.** A laboratory system has an external observer and laboratory time. What extra assumptions are needed to transfer an internal-clock model to a universe for which no external clock exists?

**Q6 — Gravity connection.** How does this operational description of time relate to the "problem of time" in canonical quantum gravity, including the Wheeler–DeWitt framework? This remains theoretical background, not evidence for GL-007 magnetic shielding.

## 6. A low-cost research path (no equipment required initially)

1. Read the original paper and identify the exact entropy quantity, observable, and definition of internal time; distinguish them from thermodynamic entropy production.
2. Model an elementary isolated two-level or oscillator system. Compare an external time parameter, a changing internal observable, and fine-grained entropy, which can remain constant under unitary evolution.
3. Simulate a small harmonic crystal near equilibrium. Compare time-independent macroscopic averages against evolving time-correlation functions; note the distinction between stationary statistics and frozen microscopic motion.
4. Construct a counterexample in which an entropy-like indicator remains constant while another observable changes; record whether the internal clock fails or whether an actual physical prediction differs.
5. Formulate rival interpretations and, if possible, a measurable discriminant; do not count a choice of clock coordinate alone as experimental proof of a new law.

### Success criterion

A useful result must specify the observable, system boundaries, dynamics, uncertainty, and a difference in predictions or explanatory power. A philosophical restatement or an equilibrium example alone does not establish that time is emergent or fundamental.

## 7. Working conclusion from the dialogue

A frozen block of ice is **macroscopically stable, not outside time**. Even at equilibrium, entropy need not be zero, and measurable microscopic dynamics may continue. When the changes used to define a particular internal clock vanish, **that clock ceases to provide time readings**. Whether fundamental time exists independently is a separate, unresolved question.
